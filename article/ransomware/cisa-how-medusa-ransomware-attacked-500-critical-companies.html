A CISA, FBI and HHS joint advisory warns of the TTPs of Medusa ransomware actors, as OPSWAT's James Neilson blames lack of IT threat awareness within OT

Having claimed over 500 victims from the critical infrastructure industries, the Medusa ransomware actors have been actively attacking organisations since 2021 until at least until April 2026. 


The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) has released new information regarding the tactics, techniques & procedures (TTPs) of this infamous ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) group. 

The joint advisory is part of a collective response under the #StopRansomware effort by the FBI, CISA and the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS).

Medusa actors are known to recruit initial access brokers (IABs) from darknet forums and marketplaces for as little as US$100 up to US$1m.

Like most threat actors, Medusa also relies on phishing campaigns as its primary method of stealing credentials. 

Unpatched software is another big entry point for the Medusa actors, even exploiting new vulnerabilities within 24 hours of disclosure. 


“The fact that around 200 organisations have been attacked in the past year proves that we're seeing critical infrastructure become an increasingly preferred target for threat actors,” says Jon Abbott, CEO and Co-Founder at ThreatAware. 

“Medusa ransomware are well known for seeking out weaker points of entry into organisations to then move laterally to launch their attack on target systems. 

“When considering the possibilities for disruption that an attack can cause, it's no surprise that these critical sectors are being targeted.”

How they quietly live off the land

Once a victim is compromised, persistence is established by creating a “domain account to maintain access to victim systems”.

Stealth is paramount to stay hidden and live off the land as long as possible, which explains why Medusa deploys a number of tactics to stay hidden. 

They have been known to cover their tracks by deleting PowerShell command line history.

Medusa actors have abused the legitimate Windows comsvcs.dll component to create an Local Security Authority Subsystem Service (LSASS) memory dump – allowing them to extract credentials while potentially evading detection by avoiding dedicated credential-dumping tools.

They are also known to deploy payloads which are obfuscated base64 strings read into memory. 


Another interesting technique deployed by the group is to place tooling like Rclone – which is used to exfiltrate data – within pre-existing Windows Defender excluded folders to prevent discovery.  

Medusa actors have affected industries in medical, education, legal, insurance, technology and manufacturing sectors. 

“The risk of healthcare or financial systems poses a huge threat to safety and so they’re often more likely to pay a ransom in hopes of being able to keep running smoothly,” Jon notes.

He says that visibility across the entire inventory is crucial. 

Medusa ransomware group’s modus operandi is to seek privileged access and then move laterally to find valuable systems on which to deploy the ransomware

“This then makes the job of patching security gaps and isolating unauthorised access within the network, as advised by CISA, much easier,” Jon says, “Without complete asset visibility, critical infrastructure organisations are taking risks they simply cannot afford and leaving gaps for Medusa to exploit.” 


Lateral movement and execution 

As is usually the case with cybercriminal groups, Medusa leverages a host of legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) tools for its nefarious activities. 

To stay under the radar, they go for tools that are already present in the victim’s environment. 

The FBI has found Medusa to use AnyDesk, Atera, ConnectWise, eHorus, N-able, BeyondTrust, SimpleHelp and Splashtop.

Medusa uses the above tools along with Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) to move laterally across the network, identifying files for encryption and exfiltration. 

“Medusa ransomware group’s modus operandi is to seek privileged access and then move laterally to find valuable systems on which to deploy the ransomware,” notes James Neilson, SVP of Global Sales at OPSWAT.

“IT systems, internet connectivity and transient devices remain major attack surfaces for ICS/OT infrastructure and Medusa ransomware looks to exploit these.”


Operating a leak site on Tor like most extortion groups, Medusa uses a double-extortion model, where victims are to pay for both decryption of their files and again to prevent the public release.  

“One of the major factors in why Medusa ransomware has been so successful in breaching critical infrastructure organisations is that there’s a lack of understanding among security teams regarding the impact of IT threats on OT environments,” James says. 

“IT security controls are often directly applied to OT systems, which creates a false sense of security and causes disruptive false positives.
