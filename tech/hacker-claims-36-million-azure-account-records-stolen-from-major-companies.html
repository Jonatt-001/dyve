Threat Actor Claims Sale of 3.6 Million Azure Employee Records From Major Global Companies

A threat actor operating under the alias “TheHatman” is advertising millions of employee records allegedly extracted from the Microsoft Azure and Entra ID environments of several major global companies, claiming the information was obtained using compromised credentials.

The alleged datasets, which began appearing on cybercrime forums on July 31, reportedly include employee directories attributed to organizations such as McDonald’s, Gap Inc., Vodafone, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), HCL Technologies, InterContinental Hotels Group (IHG), Kyndryl, Hexaware Technologies, and Wyndham Hotels & Resorts.

Across the listings, TheHatman claims to possess approximately 3.6 million records. The largest advertised dataset is allegedly linked to McDonald’s, with approximately 1.7 million employee records.

However, the claims remain under investigation. While cybersecurity researchers say samples of the advertised datasets display characteristics consistent with corporate Azure/Entra directory exports, the affected companies have not universally confirmed that their systems were breached, and the precise method used to obtain the information remains unclear.

McDonald’s Allegedly Largest Dataset

In one of the most recent listings, TheHatman claims to be selling an internal McDonald’s employee database containing approximately 1.7 million records.

The threat actor claims the information was:

“downloaded directly from Azure Tenant using compromised credentials.”

According to the listing, the dataset contains information including employee names, employee IDs, corporate email addresses, job titles, telephone numbers, postal addresses, service accounts and other tenant-related account information.

The significance of the alleged dataset goes beyond the exposure of individual employee contact information.

If authentic and sufficiently current, a large corporate directory can provide an attacker with a detailed map of an organization’s workforce, including potentially valuable information about employees, departments, account structures and privileged identities.

That information can subsequently be used to improve phishing campaigns, impersonation attempts and targeted social-engineering operations.

TCS Disputes the Alleged Breach

The second-largest dataset advertised by TheHatman is allegedly associated with Tata Consultancy Services, with more than 800,000 employee records.

As with the McDonald’s listing, the threat actor claims the information was obtained directly from an Azure tenant using compromised credentials.

TCS, however, has disputed the implication that its systems were breached.

In a notification to India’s National Stock Exchange, the company said it investigated the claims and found no credible evidence of a breach of TCS systems or customer environments.

The company also said the information appeared to be at least four years old and consisted primarily of basic employee information.

According to TCS, the threat actor claimed password spraying and MFA fatigue were used as attack techniques. The company said it had maintained safeguards against those techniques for more than two years and that its review found those defenses remained effective.

The distinction is important: the existence of a dataset containing legitimate historical employee information does not, by itself, establish that an attacker recently compromised the company’s production environment.

Gap Says Data Is Old and Non-Sensitive

Gap Inc. has similarly rejected the suggestion that its corporate systems were compromised.

A company spokesperson said its preliminary investigation found that the advertised information was limited in scope, non-sensitive and several years old.

The company also said there was no evidence that its corporate systems had been compromised.

That response introduces another important question surrounding the campaign: whether the datasets represent fresh intrusions, historical information obtained during previous compromises, legitimately accessible directory information, or a combination of different sources.

The age of the information is particularly relevant because corporate identity directories can remain useful to attackers even after the underlying accounts have been deactivated or organizational structures have changed.

Researchers Find Signs of Corporate Directory Exports

Cybercrime intelligence company Hudson Rock analyzed samples associated with the listings and found what it described as “foundational corporate directory attributes.”

The datasets reportedly contain structured information associated with corporate identity environments, including employee names, email addresses, organizational information and active domains.

Researchers also identified tenant-specific .onmicrosoft.com structures, along with service accounts and references to global administrators.

Those details are significant because they can expose the internal structure of an organization rather than simply providing a static list of employees.

A directory containing relationships between employees, departments, groups, service accounts and privileged accounts can provide an attacker with a ready-made reconnaissance resource.

For example, an attacker could potentially identify:

  • Employees in security or IT roles.
  • Administrators and other privileged identities.
  • Corporate email naming conventions.
  • Department structures.
  • Service accounts.
  • Organizational relationships.
  • Potential targets for highly personalized phishing campaigns.
  • Domains and tenant information that can assist further reconnaissance.

The information could therefore have value even if the datasets do not contain passwords or direct access credentials.

How Were the Records Obtained?

This remains one of the biggest unanswered questions.

TheHatman claims that compromised credentials were used to access the affected Azure tenants. In some listings, the actor has referenced techniques including password spraying and MFA fatigue.

However, the exact intrusion chain has not been independently established.

Hudson Rock has reported finding compromised Microsoft credentials associated with several of the organizations and has suggested that stolen credentials may be relevant to the campaign. Other possible explanations have not been completely ruled out.

Potential scenarios could include compromised credentials, stolen authentication material, phishing, infostealer infections or abuse of an overly privileged account or integration.

Importantly, the available evidence does not establish that Microsoft Azure itself was compromised through a common Azure vulnerability.

The distinction between an Azure infrastructure vulnerability and unauthorized access to individual customer tenants is critical.

A cloud platform can remain operationally secure while an attacker uses a legitimate but compromised identity to access information that the identity is permitted to see.

Why Directory Data Can Be Dangerous

At first glance, employee names, email addresses and job titles may appear relatively low-risk compared with passwords, financial records or customer databases.

The problem is the relationship between the records.

A corporate directory can reveal how an organization is structured and who holds important positions.

When information about employees is combined with administrative roles, service accounts, domains and organizational relationships, it can become an intelligence package for subsequent attacks.

An attacker does not necessarily need a password contained in the database.

Knowing the identity of a company’s administrators, security personnel, executives and technical staff can make subsequent social-engineering attempts substantially more convincing.

For example, an attacker could use legitimate organizational information to construct a targeted phishing message that appears to originate from an employee’s manager, security team or internal IT department.

The same information could also be useful for identifying high-value accounts that warrant additional reconnaissance.

This is why the alleged exposure is more significant than a conventional employee-directory leak.

Authentication May Be the Real Story

If TheHatman’s claims are eventually confirmed, the campaign could highlight a broader problem with identity security rather than a flaw specific to Azure.

Modern cloud environments increasingly concentrate access around identity providers. Once an attacker obtains a sufficiently privileged or sufficiently useful identity, they may not need to exploit a software vulnerability to obtain valuable information.

The security boundary becomes the account itself.

That makes protections such as phishing-resistant authentication, conditional access, device and session controls, privileged identity management, credential monitoring and rapid token/session revocation increasingly important.

MFA also should not be treated as an absolute guarantee against account compromise. Attackers can target the authentication process itself, while stolen sessions or authentication tokens can create additional avenues for abuse.

Authenticity Still Has to Be Established

Despite the apparent structure of the samples, there is an important distinction between data authenticity and breach attribution.

A dataset can contain genuine corporate information without proving when, how or from where the information was obtained.

Similarly, an authentic employee directory does not necessarily demonstrate that the company suffered a recent intrusion.

This distinction is particularly relevant because several organizations have characterized the advertised information as old or non-sensitive.

Hudson Rock has expressed high confidence in the authenticity of elements of the data it examined, but the precise access and exfiltration mechanism remains unresolved.

BleepingComputer also reported that it was unable to independently verify the authenticity of the advertised datasets.

No Evidence Yet of a Single Azure-Wide Breach

The current evidence does not support describing the incident as a compromise of Microsoft Azure as a whole.

Instead, the emerging picture is of a threat actor claiming access to multiple individual corporate identity environments and attempting to monetize information allegedly obtained from them.

That difference matters.

If the claims are eventually validated, investigators will need to determine whether the organizations were compromised through a common technique, a common credential ecosystem, unrelated credential compromises, third-party access, or another mechanism entirely.

The answer could determine whether this represents a coordinated campaign against cloud identities or simply a collection of unrelated compromises being marketed together.

What Happens Next

The most important questions are now whether the advertised datasets can be independently validated, how recently the information was obtained, whether any credentials or authentication material were exposed, and whether the affected organizations’ identity environments were actually accessed by an unauthorized party.

Organizations named in the listings will also need to determine whether the exposed information remains current and whether privileged accounts, service accounts or authentication sessions associated with the datasets require additional investigation.

For defenders, the episode reinforces a broader lesson: identity directories are security-sensitive assets.

Even when a database contains no passwords or payment information, a detailed map of an organization’s people, roles, domains and privileged accounts can significantly reduce the amount of reconnaissance an attacker needs to perform.

For now, the claims surrounding TheHatman remain only partially verified. The advertised volumes are substantial, the samples reportedly contain meaningful corporate directory information, and researchers have found indicators consistent with genuine enterprise identity data. But the precise intrusion paths, timing and extent of any unauthorized access remain unresolved.

BleepingComputer contacted the companies named in the listings for comment. At the time of its reporting, several had not responded.

Bottom line: the story is not simply about millions of employee records allegedly being put up for sale. It is about the potential weaponization of corporate identity intelligence — and how a compromised cloud identity can expose far more organizational information than an attacker may initially appear to have accessed.
